# Assemble System One from exact code outward

System One's robust product is about a dozen duties, not one model, and the evidence says most of them are exact code or statistics rather than learned judgement: owners, regulators, brokers and operators converge on a signed mandate compiled into per-action predicates, an immutable and replayable decision-time record, a stop that works per agent, and a stated bound on how often bad actions get through, while only grounding (does this source, read for meaning, support this claim) and manipulation reading need language models. The attacker's view changes the design most, because tighten-only guarantees that a fooled reader can at worst over-hold, but the attacker wants **evasion**, a bad action that raises no signal at all, and every injection classifier and prompt-level defence tested adaptively in 2025–2026 was bypassed, most at **above 90% success**; the only defences with a structural argument against an adaptive attacker are provenance gates that hold any action whose arguments trace back to untrusted text. The best composite to run on two RTX A6000s is therefore code checks with provenance that propagates down the why graph on CPU; a single frozen, truncated, Apache-licensed open decoder of about 9B parameters on one GPU, chosen by a layer sweep, with several cheap heads that tap different layers in one forward pass, backed by an encoder cross-encoder as the fail-closed CPU fallback; a monotone EBM combiner with global temperature, conformal risk control, conformal kNN novelty and an e-detector on CPU; and an asynchronous escalation judge from a different model family from the one that wrote the training and test data. The lab blueprint's shape survives the evidence, but five of its details do not: 27B decision models do not fit one A6000 in bf16; TabPFN-class learners cannot sit in a 50 ms CPU path; numeric embeddings, pointer heads and typed cross-attention have no supporting evidence on pretrained models; one model family, DeepSeek, generates the data, authors the test worlds and judges, which inflates every agreement metric; and the product needs a per-agent kill path and two-person change control that System One's "never cancel" rule pushes to an authorisation layer that does not yet provide them. What remains uncertain is mostly measurement: no component here has an A6000 latency figure, and no decision-model, verifier or classifier score comes from an independent adaptive test on financial text.

## Seven stakeholders reduce to a dozen duties, and most are code

The stakeholder views overlap more than they differ. Each adds one or two duties the others miss, and those additions are where the lab's plan has holes.

**Owners** need proof that the agent stayed inside what they authorised. FINRA's 2026 oversight report names the failure owners fear most: agents that "may act beyond the user's actual or intended scope and authority". It names multi-step reasoning that makes outcomes "difficult to trace or explain, complicating auditability" as the second ([FINRA 2026](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/guidance/reports/2026-finra-annual-regulatory-oversight-report/gen-ai)). The closest regulatory model for "purpose fit" is the SEC Names Rule. A fund whose name implies a focus must invest **at least 80%** of assets accordingly, checked at least quarterly since 2023, and a name can still mislead when the 80% test passes ([SEC IC-24828](https://www.sec.gov/rule-release/ic-24828); [Sullivan summary](https://www.sullivanlaw.com/viewpoints/sec-adopts-amendments-to-the-investment-company-names-rule-rule-35d-1)). That logic is computable. The lab's own check found 6,092 real funds testable and 191 below the 80% line on a headline test ([BLUEPRINT](../lab/BLUEPRINT.md)). So the owner-facing core is a compiled mandate checked per action, plus an explanation that quotes the clause and the evidence. A third owner need follows from Knight Capital: that system sent **97 alert emails before the open**, and nobody acted on them ([Finance Magnates](https://www.financemagnates.com/forex/regulation/a-costly-error-knight-capital-to-pay-12-million-to-sec-as-penalty-for-catastrophic-2012-trading-incident/)). The same will happen to owners who receive too many asks. They need an interruption budget: a rationed rate of asks, with holds that resolve without them where the mandate allows.

**Regulators** bind brokers and investment firms, not supervisor vendors, and that limits what OrderGuard can claim. SEC Rule 15c3-5 requires automated pre-trade controls under the broker's "direct and exclusive control" ([FINRA market access](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/key-topics/market-access)). An external supervisor therefore cannot satisfy 15c3-5 itself. It can only feed evidence into the broker's controls, so its records have to be exportable and keepable. MiFID II RTS 6 requires a kill switch that withdraws all *unexecuted* orders, plus conformance and stress testing and annual self-assessment, and outsourcing does not remove the duty ([RTS 6](https://ec.europa.eu/finance/securities/docs/isd/mifid/rts/160719-rts-6_en.pdf); [Kroll](https://www.kroll.com/en/publications/financial-compliance-regulation/algorithmic-trading-under-mifid-ii)). Knight Capital was the first enforcement action under 15c3-5, settled for **$12M**. The cause was a deployment: old code left live on one of eight servers, with no second-person review ([SEC 2013-222](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2013-222)). For a judgement layer, the lesson is that changes to compiled rules, thresholds and calibration maps are control changes, and they need two-person review.

**Brokers** already run fat-finger, collar, credit and rate checks. They check orders, not reasons, and their kill switches stop a session or an account, not one agent among many on the same account. Per-key and per-strategy kill switches appear only as vendor claims ([Gravitee, vendor](https://gravitee.io/corpus/gen-1134/electronic-trading/pre-trade-risk-controls-and-kill-switches-for-electronic-trading-apis.html)). The duties brokers leave open are a per-agent stop, per-agent throttles, self-trade prevention across agents on one account, and an order tag that ties each order to its decision record.

**Agent builders** have only payment-style guardrails today: spend caps, allowlists and wallet freezes enforced outside the model. All of these are vendor claims ([Policy Layer](https://policylayer.com/glossary/financial-guardrail)). The recurring design rule is to judge the transaction, not the agent's justification, because an injected agent can justify anything ([coincub, opinion](https://coincub.com/?p=14039)). That supports the blueprint's code-first order. It also says builders need what no incumbent offers: evidence-linked judgement against mandate semantics, with a predictable false-hold rate.

**Operators** need the same things FINRA asks for: monitoring of "prompts, outputs, and model versions, with logs" ([FINRA 2026](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/guidance/reports/2026-finra-annual-regulatory-oversight-report/gen-ai)). They also need decision-time snapshots for replay, calibration tracked against owner rulings, alarms on hold and ask rates, and kill-switch drills. A kill switch that has never been exercised is a failure mode in its own right.

**Scale** turns these duties into arithmetic. A million decisions a day is **11.6 per second** on average, or about 120 per second at a tenfold peak around the open or a news burst. Order volume also varies widely between agents: one Alpha Arena model placed 1,418 trades in a round where the top model placed 158 ([besthub summary](https://www.besthub.dev/articles/can-llms-trade-crypto-profitably-inside-the-alpha-arena-competition-ace3b75ceaa5)). So most decisions must clear in code, a judgement model must never sit on the per-order path, and failure must default to hold. The blueprint's rule to "judge theses; check orders in code" (D4) is the right answer to this ([BLUEPRINT](../lab/BLUEPRINT.md)).

The **attacker** view, covered in the next section, adds provenance and taint, signed-channel authority, memory provenance and turnover budgets.

|---|---|---|---|

## Tighten-only stops loosening, but attackers want evasion

The blueprint argues that a score-only reader "can be fooled by adversarial wording, but not commanded" ([BLUEPRINT §6.3](../lab/BLUEPRINT.md)). That is true, and it matters for loosening: a monotone combiner plus a judge that can only tighten means injected text can cause a hold, never a breach. But a fooled reader is the attack. An attacker who profits from a trade needs every risk signal to stay low, and monotonicity offers no protection against suppressed signals.

**The adaptive-attack record is bleak for anything that reads text.** "The Attacker Moves Second" bypassed 12 recent defences using gradient, reinforcement-learning, search and human attacks. Most fell at **above 90% success** after originally reporting near-zero. The ProtectAI detector and PromptGuard were both above 90%, and the best performer, PIGuard, still fell at 71% ([arXiv 2510.09023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09023)). AutoDojo (June 2026) ran a black-box adaptive attacker against ten defences on five models. It found that static benchmarks "significantly overestimate defence efficacy". PIGuard, for instance, went from 0.0% static to 28.0% adaptive attack success on GPT-4o-mini ([AutoDojo](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.15057v3)). The classifiers also miss injections embedded in tool output. One secondary report found PromptGuard 2 catching **6 of 629** such injections ([Ecosistema Startup](https://ecosistemastartup.com/prompt-guard-2-de-meta-detecta-1-de-ataques-reales-a-ai-agents/)). Finance makes this worse. FinVault reports attack success up to 50% in regulatory-case sandboxes, with general-purpose defences "largely ineffective" ([arXiv 2601.07853](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.07853)). TradeTrap shows that a perturbation to a single component propagates to concentration near 100% and runaway exposure ([arXiv 2512.02261](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.02261)). Many trading attacks also need no instruction at all: they bias the inputs, so an injection-string detector has nothing to find.

**Structural information-flow control is the only class with an argument that does not depend on the attacker failing to find a string.** CaMeL separates a planner that sees only the trusted request from a quarantined reader of untrusted data. It solved 77% of AgentDojo tasks with provable security, against 84% undefended ([arXiv 2503.18813](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18813)). FIDES labels every message and tool result for integrity and enforces policies deterministically. Its authors report that it stops all AgentDojo injections with policy checks on, and that every planner falls with them off ([arXiv 2505.23643](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.23643v2)). Neither has been evaluated adaptively, and CaMeL carried the largest clean-utility cost in AutoDojo ([AutoDojo](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.15057v3)). For a supervisor that only holds, though, the utility tax is missed trades, not bad trades.

The why graph is already most of a FIDES-style label system. Documents carry trust tiers, notes carry an author kind and a signature, and claims cite quotes. What is missing is **taint propagation**. If a memory note summarises an external post, it should inherit the post's low-integrity label. Then any order whose symbol, size, side or price depends on a tainted node holds, whatever the readers say. MINJA's query-only memory injection reaches above 95% injection success, and later versions above 70% attack success ([arXiv 2503.03704](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.03704v2)). That is exactly the path taint propagation closes, and it costs microseconds of graph traversal in code.

**Monitors should read the action against the trusted mandate, not the agent's narrative.** MonitorBench finds that more capable models are harder to monitor through their chain of thought ([arXiv 2603.28590](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.28590v1)). A 2026 preprint argues that chain-of-thought monitoring is unreliable when the behaviour is driven by context rather than an explicit instruction ([alphaXiv 2608.04735](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.04735)), which is the TradeTrap pattern. Meta's layered setup is the best static evidence for combining a classifier with an auditor. PromptGuard 2 plus the AlignmentCheck auditor cut AgentDojo attack success from 17.6% to 1.75%, at a utility cost from 0.477 to 0.427 ([LlamaFirewall](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.03574)). Those are fixed-attack numbers, though. In practice the cheap classifiers belong in the combiner as monotone, hold-only features, never as gates. The escalation judge should see quoted text only as labelled data, and it should be asked whether the action is consistent with the mandate, not whether the story is convincing.

**Two blueprint evaluation choices need to change.** First, C3 ("0 violations across ≥ 500 red-team cases plus a monotonicity certificate") tests the property that is already structural. The property that needs testing is evasion. The evaluation should report the success rate of an adaptive, black-box attacker at a fixed attempt budget against the full stack, AutoDojo-style, because a real attacker needs only one success. Second, one model family runs through generation, test-world authoring, teacher labels and the judge candidates. The blueprint already flags "same-lab authors (all DeepSeek) share blind spots" as a risk. The evidence makes it a contradiction. LLM evaluators prefer outputs that resemble their own ([Panickssery et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076v1)). LLMs also herd toward fabricated analyst ratings regardless of size ([arXiv 2605.09106](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.09106)). So a DeepSeek judge scored on DeepSeek-authored worlds reports inflated agreement, and a fleet judged by one family shares one failure. At least one held-out world and the production judge must come from a different family from the generator.

A cheap metamorphic test also follows from TradeTrap. Reverse the directional cues in the quoted evidence and confirm that every reader score moves the right way. The idea is an inference from the paper, not something the paper tested. It catches readers that respond to tone rather than support.

Once the duties are sorted by what they compute, the architecture follows. The table maps each function to its architecture class, where it runs and its tempo. The GPU budget is spent only where text must be read for meaning.

**The scale arithmetic favours this split.** Suppose escalation hits the blueprint's 15% ceiling on a million decisions a day. That is 150,000 judge calls a day. At the frontier prices Artificial Analysis measured ($1,530–$6,240 per million tasks), the bill is roughly **$230–$940 a day**. At 11–33 seconds per call, the system needs about 20–57 calls in flight on average and roughly ten times that at peaks ([AA Decision Index v2](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/decision-index-v2)). That is affordable asynchronously. But every escalated action holds for half a minute, so the operational target should sit well below the 15% kill line. Escalations are also not owner asks. The ask rate needs its own budget, set by the Knight lesson about ignored alerts, not by cost.

**The memory arithmetic rules out the blueprint's implied 27B reader on one card.** The reader-component notes suggest that a 27B model "fits in bf16 on one card". It does not. 27B parameters at 2 bytes each are about 54 GB, above an A6000's 48 GB. Running 27B needs either 8-bit weights (about 27 GB) or tensor parallelism across both cards, which spends the whole GPU budget on one reader. A 9B model in bf16 takes about 18 GB, and truncating it at an intermediate layer shrinks that further. For classification, 4-bit quantisation had a negligible effect in the EMNLP 2025 comparison ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.9)). So quantised 27B is a legitimate probe subject, but a 9B-class model is the realistic inline reader. The one independent cloud measurement of a 27B decision model, Clef at **0.50 s per decision** ([Arize](https://arize.com/blog/decision-model-benchmark.md)), already exceeds the 300 ms target, although a secondary source reports a 209 ms median ([Developers Digest](https://www.developersdigest.tech/blog/cloudflare-clef-decision-models-2026)). No A6000 latency exists for any candidate, so C6 has to be measured. Scoring without generation helps: every reader question is answered from one prefill pass, with no token-by-token decoding.

The proposed hardware layout therefore puts the primary reader backbone and its heads on GPU 0. GPU 1 holds a second-family reader, or the shadow candidate, plus LoRA training runs. Everything statistical stays on CPU in the harness process, which also gives the fail-closed fallback when a GPU is down: code plus the combiner, at a tighter threshold, as the blueprint already specifies.

### Pairings with evidence, and pairings without it

**A head on intermediate-layer features of a frozen backbone is the best-supported pairing.** Across 32 embedding tasks, intermediate layers consistently beat the final layer as frozen features ([Skean et al., ICML 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02013)). On the two tasks closest to F5, content safety and prompt-injection classification, a penalised logistic regression on the best intermediate layer reached the highest F1 with the fewest training examples. The best layer differed by task ([arXiv 2412.13435](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.13435v1)). Truncation is supported too, with a condition attached. Llama-family QA performance stays roughly flat up to about 40% layer removal, but only after a short "healing" fine-tune ([Gromov et al., ICLR 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17887)), and ShortGPT keeps about 90% of performance after removing about 25% of parameters ([arXiv 2403.03853](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03853)). Both results are on QA and generation, not on claim-evidence pairs. For a frozen probe, though, truncating above the deepest useful layer loses nothing by construction: the cut layers never feed the head.

Together these give the composite to build first. Take one forward pass through a backbone truncated at the deepest layer any task needs. Tap each task's best layer, which the layer sweep finds per head. Then attach small heads: supported, contradicted, out of context, wrong period or entity, hype, authority claim, and addresses the reviewer. Every reader question then costs a single prefill. One inference goes further, though no paper tests it: train several heads per question with different seeds or bootstrap samples and you get an ensemble at almost no extra cost. That matters, because ensembles of about five members were the most robust calibration method under shift in Ovadia et al. ([NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2019/hash/8558cb408c1d76621371888657d2eb1d-Abstract.html)), and a five-member ensemble of full models would cost five times the GPU.

**Label budgets are small enough for heads.** Specialised models needed about **100 labels** on average to break even with general LLMs on text classification. Variance raised that by 100–200%, and binary tasks needed more ([EMNLP 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.9)). Plan for a few hundred hard, diverse examples per head and measure the spread across seeds. SetFit-style contrastive tuning of a sentence encoder is the cheap, proven route for the CPU fallback ([arXiv 2209.11055](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11055)).

**Light tuning is a second stage, not the default.** LoRA's forgetting and out-of-distribution behaviour depend on rank, alpha and learning rate. In some settings LoRA forgets *more* than full fine-tuning, through "intruder dimensions" ([arXiv 2410.21228](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.21228v3)). DoRA adds gains at no inference cost, but they are measured on generative commonsense benchmarks ([arXiv 2402.09353](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.09353v4)). LoRA on the layers below the tap point, with a rank and learning-rate sweep scored on held-out worlds, is therefore an experiment that has to beat the frozen probe. The blueprint's kill rule (drop the tuned verifier unless it beats the frozen probe by ≥ 0.03 AUROC on held-out worlds) is the right gate. Averaging the weights of fine-tunes from different seeds or settings ("model soups") improved out-of-distribution robustness in vision at zero inference cost ([arXiv 2203.05482](https://huggingface.co/papers/2203.05482.md)), so it belongs in that stage too.

**Distillation should feed labels and rationales into training, never rationales into inference.** In distilling step-by-step, a 770M T5 trained on labels plus LLM rationales as a second task matched the baseline on about 80% of the data, with no inference cost from the rationales ([arXiv 2305.02301](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.02301)). Rationales therefore belong as an auxiliary loss while training the heads. Escalation-judge catches, labelled as teacher data and down-weighted against code labels, are how escalations should shrink over time, as the blueprint proposes. On-policy distillation does not help label outputs, because the teacher can score the label distribution directly, with no rollouts.

**RL is the wrong tool for label heads, which confirms the blueprint over this programme's earlier recommendation.** RLCR's calibration gains are real but were measured on maths and QA with generated confidence, and out-of-domain calibration error stays high ([arXiv 2507.16806](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16806)). RLVR makes decision-oriented models "extremely overconfident" ([arXiv 2601.13284](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.13284)). For a discrete head trained with cross-entropy, temperature scaling on a held-out split is the cheaper route. RL stays an option only for the generative escalation judge.

**Several X6 ingredients have no evidence on pretrained models, and the safest move is to route around them.** The proposal feeds numbers in through a numeric embedding. xVal's continuous number tokens were shown only in models trained from scratch on scientific data ([arXiv 2310.02989](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02989)). Meanwhile, numeric NLI is a known failure: nine published NLI models failed to beat a majority-class baseline on EQUATE's quantity tests ([CoNLL 2019](https://aclanthology.org/K19-1033)). The better split is to let code compare value, unit, period and entity against the quote-anchored fact table, as F2 and F3 already do. The reader then gets only those comparison results as typed tokens (for example "unit matches: no") and the question that remains about meaning: guidance or actual, "could" or "will", whether the quote is lifted out of context. Claim-evidence cross-attention slots and pointer heads were not evidenced either. The standard way to get a pointer without one is to score each (claim, candidate fact or sentence) pair with the cross-encoder and take the arg-max. The deciding candidate is then the explanation, it is supervised by the planted cause, and it needs no new architecture. Converting a decoder to bidirectional attention needs an adaptation step, because the switch alone hurts representations ([LLM2Vec](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.05961)). That is another reason to probe the causal decoder as it is, with the claim placed after the evidence.

One hypothesis is cheap to test and worth testing. Decision models such as Clef-flash were post-trained to score typed options. Their intermediate layers may therefore separate supported from unsupported claims better than a base model of the same size. The layer sweep should include a decision-tuned and a base backbone of matched size to find out.

## The decision layer is classical ML inside statistical wrappers

**Monotone additive scoring is the right combiner, provided monotonicity is checked, not assumed.** EBMs take a per-feature `monotone_constraints` argument ([InterpretML](https://interpret.ml/docs/python/api/ExplainableBoostingRegressor.html)), and LightGBM and XGBoost support monotone trees ([XGBoost](https://xgboost.readthedocs.io/en/latest/tutorials/monotonic.html)). Credit-risk work finds that well-specified monotone constraints cost little accuracy ([arXiv 2512.17945](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.17945v2)), and monotone NAMs were built for regulatory acceptance ([arXiv 2209.10070](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2209.10070)). The pitfall bears directly on System One. If a correlated feature is left unconstrained, the model can use it to undo the constraint on its twin ([interpret issue #184](https://www.github.com/interpretml/interpret/issues/184)). Reader scores that correlate with code features are the obvious route. So every input gets a monotone-increasing constraint, including novelty, owner offsets and classifier flags. The blueprint's certificate by enumerating shape functions must also cover the two-dimensional interaction terms, not only the main effects. A perturbation test that raises one feature at a time and asserts the score never falls is the cheap complement.

**Calibration must keep the ranking.** A single global temperature preserves order, and therefore monotonicity. An isotonic map is monotone by construction. Input-dependent temperature, the fix for multi-domain miscalibration ([NeurIPS 2022](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/b054fadf1ccd80b37d465f6082629934-Paper-Conference.pdf)), can break the signed-contribution property unless it is constrained. Fit on a held-out set that spans owners and agent types, and refit when the calibration monitor fires.

**Conformal risk control fits the false-allow bound, but its premise is exactly what an adaptive agent breaks.** CRC controls the expected value of any monotone loss with finite-sample validity ([arXiv 2208.02814](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02814)). The loss "allowed and the owner would reject" is monotone in the threshold, so CRC applies directly, and learn-then-test covers non-monotone variants ([arXiv 2110.01052](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.01052v4)). Both assume calibration and live data are exchangeable. Weighted conformal needs the likelihood ratio, and its guarantee degrades with estimation error ([arXiv 1904.06019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06019)). Adaptive conformal inference keeps only long-run coverage ([arXiv 2106.00170](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.00170)). The blueprint's α = 0.05 per action class is sound if it is stated as "while the drift monitor passes". The decision record should store the calibration-set provenance and the monitor state in force, so that a regulator reads the bound together with its assumption. Owner-level (Mondrian) bounds shrink each calibration set to one owner's labels. Use a pooled bound plus a per-owner shift check until owners have enough labels.

**Owner adaptation needs partial pooling on the request path, with tabular foundation models kept offline.** TabPFN-2.5 reports strong few-shot results ([arXiv 2511.08667](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.08667)). But Prior Labs itself says CPU inference is slow, usable only for small datasets, and recommends a GPU, with most fitting happening inside each prediction call ([Prior Labs FAQ](https://docs.priorlabs.ai/faq.md)). TabICL is faster than TabPFN v2 ([arXiv 2502.05564](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.05564)), but one practitioner measured about 15 s on CPU ([mindfulmodeler](https://mindfulmodeler.substack.com/p/making-tabular-foundation-models)). That puts the blueprint's X4 "in-context tabular learner" arm outside the 50 ms CPU path. It can supply a prior or pseudo-labels offline, or be distilled. The live mechanism is a per-owner offset with a shrinkage prior toward the population, entered monotonically and allowed to loosen only within the signed mandate. No public investor-preference dataset exists, so owners must be simulated from personas and checked against a small real sample.

**Novelty and drift each need their own monitor, and each monitor should name what fired.** No tabular out-of-distribution benchmark settles the score to use. EBM-based detectors showed no consistent edge on tabular data ([arXiv 2107.08785](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.08785v1.pdf)). A kNN or Mahalanobis distance over standardised features plus the reader's pooled representation, turned into a split-conformal p-value, gives a calibrated alert rate and fits the CPU budget. That supports the blueprint's F12. Adversarial perturbation can raise a learned conformal novelty detector's false-discovery rate ([arXiv 2510.00463](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2510.00463)), so novelty must enter as one more monotone risk signal and never as a bypass. For drift, the blueprint's CUSUM works: in OrderGuard's benchmark, the style-distance plus CUSUM monitor caught 100% of drifting agents with no false alarms, even on the shifted set where the learned model fell to 0.634 AUROC ([JUDGEMENT.md](../JUDGEMENT.md)). E-detectors add an anytime-valid bound on false alarms with a guaranteed average run length ([edetect](https://cran.r-universe.dev/edetect/doc/manual.html)), which is the property an operator or regulator can quote. Run one on the score stream (calibration drift) and one on the feature stream (covariate shift).

## Six stacks to race, and the one to run

The parts above combine into six stacks. Each adds one thing to the one before, so every comparison isolates one decision. All of them share S0 as the floor.

| Stack | Composition | Hardware | What it decides | Keep or kill |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **S0 Exact core** | F1–F3 with taint propagation; monotone EBM; global temperature; CRC; conformal kNN novelty; e-detector plus CUSUM | CPU | How far code and statistics go alone | Baseline: every reader must beat it on held-out worlds at equal hold rate |
| **S1 Encoder heads** | S0 + Ettin-400M or ModernBERT-large cross-encoder, contrastively tuned with multiple heads and a pairwise arg-max pointer; PromptGuard 2 and ProtectAI as hold-only features; GLiNER2.5-Decide zero-shot as reference | CPU or GPU 1 | Whether a cheap encoder carries to new wording | Becomes the fail-closed fallback if it beats S0 |
| **S2 Frozen truncated-decoder probes** | S0 + layer sweep over a decision-tuned 9B (Clef-flash), a base decoder of similar size and, for probing only, a 27B at 8-bit; logistic or MLP heads on each task's best layer; truncate at the deepest tap; five-head ensembles | GPU 0 | Which backbone and layer represent "supported" and "manipulative" best | Expected primary reader; must meet C1 and C6 |
| **S3 Lightly tuned truncated decoder** | S2 + LoRA or DoRA below the tap, with a rank and learning-rate sweep; healing pass; seed soup; rationale auxiliary loss from the judge | GPU 0 (train on GPU 1) | Whether tuning adds anything over probing | Kill unless +0.03 AUROC over S2 on held-out worlds |
| **S4 Decision model as reader** | S0 + Clef-flash typed questions, probabilities locally recalibrated and entered as monotone features | GPU 0 | Whether a vendor decision head beats our own heads | Switch to it if +0.05 AUROC on unseen wording, as in the blueprint's switching rule |
| **S5 Cascade** | The best of S1–S4 + an asynchronous judge from a family not used to generate data or author worlds; verdict parsed and allowed only to tighten; its catches distilled into S3 | External | Whether escalation adds recall on novel failures | Lower the 15% cap toward a few percent; if the judge adds < 0.10 recall, drop it |

**The configuration to run, if the evidence holds, is S0 + S2 (or S3) on GPU 0 + S1 as the CPU fallback + S5 for the tail.** S2 is the favourite because its pairing has the most direct evidence: an intermediate-layer probe won on prompt injection with the fewest labels. It is also the cheapest to retrain when a new failure family appears, and it answers every reader question in one pass. S4 earns a lane because the decision-model category validates typed questions at sub-second latency. But no vendor has published held-out calibration, and on two A6000s its 27B variants cost a whole card each. The choice between S2 and S4 should come from the lab's held-out worlds, not from leaderboards.

**The data recipe matters more than the stack choice, and the evidence says the blueprint's synthetic labels will be too easy.** The lab's reference world already scores every model at 0.97–0.99 AUROC and cannot tell them apart. The old baseline fell from **0.974 to 0.634** under shift ([BLUEPRINT](../lab/BLUEPRINT.md)). EACL 2026 explains the pattern: LLM-generated NLI examples are often too simple, while harder synthetic examples and upsampled hard human examples improve challenging out-of-distribution sets ([Stacey et al.](https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-eacl.286)). VitaminC's minimal contrastive pairs gave about 10% on adversarial fact verification ([arXiv 2103.08541](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.08541)). The blueprint's corruption operators with hard negatives are VitaminC-shaped, which is right. They need a difficulty filter on top: keep the corruptions that S0 or the current reader gets wrong, and mix in human-written hard cases.

Licences decide which sources can be used for what. FinQA (MIT on its repository) can serve as a trainable base for corruptions ([GitHub](https://github.com/czyssrs/FinQA)), and VitaminC can be trained on subject to its share-alike terms ([HF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/tals/vitaminc/blob/main/README.md)). LLM-AggreFact ([HF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/lytang/LLM-AggreFact/blob/main/README.md)) and TRAIL ([HF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/PatronusAI/TRAIL/blob/main/README.md)) restrict use to evaluation, which makes them good independent test sets and nothing more. FinanceBench's open sample is CC BY-NC and cannot feed a commercial model. AgentDojo (MIT) and BIPIA cover injection testing, while fake authority and financial hype are covered by no public set and must be generated, then checked against a small human-labelled sample.

**Evaluation should report what each stakeholder will ask.** For owners and regulators: false-allow rate with its CRC upper bound, coverage of endorsed actions (so that tightening cannot hide), and calibration per held-out world, against the in-distribution and shifted targets (D5). For attackers: adaptive black-box attack success at a fixed attempt budget, the cue-reversal metamorphic test, and framing-symmetry rates. For operators: false holds per thousand actions at fixed recall, escalation rate, ask rate per owner, and p99 latency measured on the A6000s and the production CPU. For scale: the same metrics under a tenfold burst replay.

## Conclusion

The question "which model?" turns out to be the least important design choice. The stakeholder duties that carry legal and commercial weight are exact: a compiled and signed mandate, provenance with taint, a per-agent stop, replayable records, change control and a bounded false-allow rate. They run on CPU and can be proven. The learned part narrows to two reading questions over short, typed inputs. The parts with the most evidence behind them, a frozen intermediate-layer probe with cheap multi-head ensembles, cost one prefill on one 48 GB card. Everything above that (tuning, vendor decision heads, frontier judges) is a lane in a race that the held-out worlds should decide.

The sharper shift is in what "robust" means. Tighten-only makes System One safe to deploy, but it does not make it hard to evade. Every published defence that reads text has failed under adaptive attack. The defensible product claim is therefore not "our reader catches manipulation". It is "no action whose arguments came from untrusted text executes without a hold, and every other allowance sits under a stated bound while the drift monitor passes". The two things that most threaten the credibility of System One's numbers are both fixable this quarter. One is the single-family pipeline that writes the data, authors the tests and judges. The other is synthetic labels too easy to separate models. Neither fix needs a new model.
