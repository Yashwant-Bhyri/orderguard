# Calibrated decision models: how they are built, evaluated and shipped (state as of November 2026)

Scope note: research done 17 November 2026. The decision-model product category is about two months old (Jev early access 15 Sep 2026, Liquid d1 29 Sep 2026), so almost all product facts are vendor claims or secondary press. The independent evidence is thin: one benchmark operator (Artificial Analysis), two single-author arXiv preprints that use Jev, and one Hugging Face community write-up. Items marked "background" come from well-known papers I know of but did not re-fetch in this session. Check them before quoting exact figures.

## 1. What do commercial decision-model products claim, and what is independently evidenced?

### Takeaway
A new product category, "System One" or decision models, takes a state plus typed questions (choice, yes/no "Noul", ordinal score, box) and returns probability distributions with no text generation. The category is real and has a third-party benchmark (Artificial Analysis Decision Index). On that benchmark, though, frontier LLMs at maximum reasoning still score higher (77 and 76 against 73). What decision models win is roughly 40–350x lower latency and 15–400x lower cost per task. Calibration claims are mostly unpublished. The one independent field study of Jev needed local recalibration, which cut calibration error 3.3x. Its authors found that calibration "varies by model rather than by paradigm".

### Cited Findings

**TypeSafe AI: Jev ("the first System One model")**
- Jev is pitched as a decision model, not a chat model. It "returns structured results directly. No text generation, no parsing." The input is a "state" (text, JSON or images) plus one or more typed questions in a single request — [TypeSafe docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/introduction)
- Question types and what each returns: Choice (choice, probabilities, confidence), Score (score, probabilities, confidence), Noul (a value in 0–1), Box (coordinates, confidence). Questions are "evaluated in parallel and in isolation against the same state", so adding questions does not cause "context rot" — [TypeSafe docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/introduction)
- The docs recommend one well-scoped question per evaluation, like a quick expert judgement. Multi-factor judgements should be split into separate questions and recombined in your own code with your own weights — [TypeSafe docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/introduction)
- The introduction page gives no training method, architecture, latency figures, pricing, benchmarks or calibration results. The only latency claim is qualitative: "Adding questions barely changes the response time" — [TypeSafe docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/introduction)
- Training is described, in secondary sources, as "Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions" (RLCD). It is framed as optimising for honest probabilities, as opposed to human preference (RLHF) or verifiable rewards (RLVR). Speed is claimed as 40–200x faster than frontier LLMs (a vendor claim). Early access opened 15 Sep 2026, and jev-1.14.0 is dated 14 Oct 2026. One secondary source says up to 255 options per choice. Images and the Box type are reportedly not production-ready yet — [DigitalOcean explainer](https://www.digitalocean.com/resources/articles/what-is-jev); [DataCamp](https://www.datacamp.com/blog/system-one-models-jev); [TrueFoundry](https://www.truefoundry.com/blog/typesafe-ai-jev)
- TypeSafe's actual RLCD recipe has not been disclosed — [Failproof AI on RLCD](https://befailproof.ai/jev/rlcd/)
- A "Jev-Ultrafast" variant also exists, cited from "published specifications" — [arXiv 2609.28940](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28940)
- **Independent use 1, police crash narratives** (Rafe & Das, arXiv 2609.24052, 21 Sep 2026). Jev screened 499,500 Texas crash narratives and coded 195,857 of them against a 27-question schema. F1 was 0.908 against human labels. **Recalibrating on the same labels cut calibration error by 3.3x.** Two frontier LLMs were benchmarked on the same records, and "calibration varies by model rather than by paradigm". The paper also notes a resolution-floor bound for any model that reports probabilities on a discrete grid, and it sizes a human review budget per variable per year. Cost scales with schema size, not narrative length. Author independence from TypeSafe is not stated — [arXiv 2609.24052](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24052)
- **Independent use 2, pentest agents** (arXiv 2609.28940). The paper describes System One models as "lightweight non-generative classifiers that return typed, calibrated verdicts". It places them at four decision points in an LLM pentest harness: finding adjudication, severity recalibration, agent pruning, and confirmation loops. The evidence is one run with Jev and one without, on a target with 13 vulnerabilities, and the differences "do not establish statistical significance". No calibration metrics are reported — [arXiv 2609.28940](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28940)

**Liquid AI: d1, d1-ultra, d1.2, d1.2-ultra**
- d1 was released 29 Sep 2026. It is proprietary and served only through a hosted API, with no weights and no fine-tuning on your own data. It has a 32k context, a free tier (`d1:free`), and unpublished paid rates. It is also served on Vercel AI Gateway as `liquid/d1`. Question types are Noul (yes/no probability), Choice (a probability per option) and Score (a weighted position on an ordinal scale). Liquid publishes no calibration evidence and tells users to measure calibration on their own traffic — [DataNorth](https://datanorth.ai/news/liquid-ai-releases-d1)
- Liquid's docs claim "Zero" output tokens and "Calibrated probabilities for every answer", with no method or measurement. They also claim fewer "verdict flips" on repeated evaluations, again unmeasured. Latency is described only as "Low and predictable". Model names `d1.2` and `d1.2-ultra` appear, and extraction and grounding questions need d1.2 or d1.2-ultra. Question types listed: Choice (including multi-select), Noul, Score, Text, Number, Date, Spans, Box, Boxes — [Liquid decision model guide](https://docs.liquid.ai/guides/decision-model-guide)
- **Notable:** Liquid's docs show the Python client as `typesafe-sdk`: `TypeSafeClient(api_key=..., base_url="https://api.liquid.ai/decisions")` and `client.system_one(model, state, questions)`. Liquid therefore exposes d1 through TypeSafe's SDK, question schema and "System One" naming. This is either a shared interface standard or a partnership. The DataNorth article does not mention it, so the relationship is unexplained — [Liquid decision model guide](https://docs.liquid.ai/guides/decision-model-guide); [DataNorth](https://datanorth.ai/news/liquid-ai-releases-d1)
- d1-ultra (15 Oct 2026, hosted, multimodal) is reported as #1 on the AA Decision Index (v1) at 65, priced at $0.042 per million input tokens. Liquid says it trained d1-ultra to improve calibration and claims the lowest calibration error in its own tests, but no study has been published. Its launch materials cite an AA-Omniscience-Decision non-hallucination rate of 85%, against 40% for Microsoft-Decision-1 — [MarkTechPost d1-ultra](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/10/15/liquid-ai-d1-ultra-decision-model/) (fetch blocked, search snippet only); [AICoder](https://aicoder.com/news/news-20261017-liquid-ai-d1-ultra-decision-model-aa-index-1); [AlphaSignal](https://alphasignal.ai/news/liquid-ai-s-d1-ultra-skips-text-generation-and-returns-raw-probabilities)
- d1.2-ultra is reported at 75 on Decision Index v2.1, first of 24 decision models, but "last on HLE" — [OrcaRouter](https://www.orcarouter.ai/blog/liquid-ai-d1-2-ultra-decision-index-v2-1). This **conflicts** with the AA v2 article (8 Nov), whose top three decision models are Drex Max, GLiDE and Mammoth, each at 72–73. A different index version (v2 vs v2.1) is the likely reason.
- Secondary reporting describes an open-weight d1 family (d1-3B, d1-omni-600M) — [NexusAI](https://www.nexusai-tech.com/ai-insights/liquid-ai-open-sources-d1-decision-models-one-step-calibrated-decisions). This **conflicts** with DataNorth's statement that d1 has no weights. It is unresolved.

**Convai Innovations: Laya (open-weight)**
- Laya is an open-weight (Apache 2.0), non-autoregressive, encoder-based decision model that runs locally and is marketed at ~33 ms per decision. Convai also uses the "RLCD" name, independently of TypeSafe — [Laya Studio](https://laya.studio/learn/what-is-laya); [El Solitario](https://elsolitario.org/en/2026/09/19/laya-convai-decision-engine-33ms/); [eesel](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/laya-ai)
- The documented Laya recipe is the only public RLCD recipe found. The policy action is a probability distribution. The reward combines log and spherical proper scores, plus a ranked probability score for ordinal questions. Exploration adds Gaussian noise to the logits. The optimiser is REINFORCE with a group-mean baseline (GRPO-like), with TD(λ=1) to credit earlier multi-turn states with eventual outcomes. Temperature scaling on held-out data is applied after training — [HF blog, Taghia](https://huggingface.co/blog/javadtaghia/rlcd-are-we-moving-from-ai-models-that-generate-an)
- Independent-ish check (one author, synthetic 2,000-decision benchmark, not head-to-head): fine-tuned Laya scored 76.6% accuracy with ECE 0.213, while Jev's published figures are 72.7% and ECE 0.144. Temperature refitting cut Laya's general checkpoint ECE from 0.466 to 0.081. The author concludes that "RLCD does not automatically guarantee calibration". They call for an ablation against plain supervised learning with the same architecture and data — [HF blog, Taghia](https://huggingface.co/blog/javadtaghia/rlcd-are-we-moving-from-ai-models-that-generate-an)

**Other vendors in the category (from the AA Decision Index v2, 8 Nov 2026)**
- Named decision models: Drex Max v1.1 (Nace AI), GLiDE v1.2 (Fastino Labs), Mammoth 27B (attributed to both "Baseten" and "Base Labs"), Elephant 9B, Decider v1.2 / Decider Flash v1.2, Clef, Celeris-1.1 Decision, and Jev 1.14 — [AA Decision Index v2](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/decision-index-v2). Fastino also released an open-weight GLiNER2.5-Decide four days before d1 — [DataNorth](https://datanorth.ai/news/liquid-ai-releases-d1). A "Microsoft-Decision-1" is mentioned in Liquid's launch comparison — [NexusAI](https://www.nexusai-tech.com/ai-insights/liquid-ai-open-sources-d1-decision-models-one-step-calibrated-decisions)
- No OpenAI, Google or Anthropic "decision endpoint" turned up in the searches run. Frontier labs appear in the index as general LLMs run at max reasoning (Claude Haiku 5.5 Max, GPT-6 Luna Max) — [AA Decision Index v2](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/decision-index-v2)

**The independent benchmark: Artificial Analysis Decision Index v2 (8 Nov 2026)**
- Decision Score per question = 50 × (1 + c − (p − c)²), where p is the probability on the chosen answer and c is correctness. Aggregated, this is 50 × (1 + Accuracy − Brier). On AA-Paperclip, a document's confidence is the product of its 15 answer probabilities — [AA v2](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/decision-index-v2)
- Components: AA-Paperclip 30% (1,500 private documents with 5 decisions and 10 facts each, all 15 must be correct); **AA-Symmetry 15%** (120 questions × 440 variants with reordered options, swapped labels and negated yes/no, testing answer and probability consistency); AA-Omniscience-Decision 15% (10 phrasings of "I don't know"); HLE-MultiChoice 15% (with a decline option); EvalSafe-O*NET-AA 15% (an implementation of TypeSafe's own O*NET benchmark); and When2Call 10% (from NVIDIA). WildGuardTest was dropped as saturated. 45% of the index is private data — [AA v2](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/decision-index-v2)
- Scores: Claude Haiku 5.5 (Max) 77, GPT-6 Luna (Max) 76, Drex Max v1.1 73, GLiDE v1.2 73, Mammoth 27B 72. Paperclip pass rates: Haiku 92%, GPT-6 Luna 86%, Drex Max 63%, GLiDE 62%, Decider 55%, Clef 36%, Celeris 24% — [AA v2](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/decision-index-v2)
- Calibration changes rankings. Celeris (24% pass) ties Clef (36% pass) on Decision Score because its Brier score is 0.15 against Clef's 0.27 — [AA v2](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/decision-index-v2)
- AA-Symmetry same-answer rate across variants: Mammoth 99%, Drex Max 91%, GLiDE 88%, Clef 67%. High symmetry scores partly reflect abstention — [AA v2](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/decision-index-v2)
- Latency per task: Celeris 56 ms, Elephant 9B 70 ms, **Jev 1.14 92 ms**, Drex Max 275 ms, GPT-6 Luna (Max) 11.34 s, Claude Haiku 5.5 (Max) 32.65 s. Cost per 1M tasks ranges from $14 (Celeris) to $102 (Drex Max), $780 (GLiDE), $1,530 (GPT-6 Luna Max) and $6,240 (Haiku Max). Billing design (input billed once per document vs per question) drives cost as much as token price does — [AA v2](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/decision-index-v2)

### Inferences
- The product shape has converged: a state plus a typed question set, a full distribution per question, and no generated text. Liquid shipping through a `typesafe-sdk` client suggests the interface is becoming a de facto standard. OrderGuard can adopt this interface (one typed question per mandate clause, combined in code) whichever paradigm sits underneath.
- "Calibrated" is the headline claim, but no vendor has published reliability diagrams or ECE on held-out real data. The best independent datapoint (crash narratives) shows out-of-box calibration was good enough to use but improved 3.3x with local recalibration. That points to vendor model plus local post-hoc recalibration as the realistic recipe.
- AA-Symmetry (reordering, label swaps, negation) is directly relevant to an adversarial trading setting. An agent could phrase the same action in different framings, and OrderGuard's judge should be tested the same way.
- The roughly 4-point accuracy gap to frontier LLMs, set against the 100x+ latency advantage, makes a cascade the natural design: a fast decision model first, escalating to a frontier LLM judge or the human review queue when confidence is low.

### Gaps
- No primary TypeSafe publication of the RLCD objective, architecture, model size or calibration curves was found. "Non-autoregressive / encoder" is documented only for Laya.
- I could not find whether d1 is built on Liquid's LFM architecture, its parameter count, or its training method.
- The open-weight vs hosted-only status of d1 conflicts across sources, and so do the Decision Index scores across versions (v1 65, v2 73, v2.1 75). I could not reach the live AA leaderboard.
- No product disclosed robustness to adversarial or injected state text.

## 2. How are models trained and post-processed for calibration?

### Takeaway
There are three levers. (a) Train for calibration by putting a bounded proper scoring rule in the RL reward: RLCR's Brier term, calibration-aware RL on decision tokens, CDPO. (b) Recalibrate after training: temperature, Platt or isotonic scaling on held-out data. (c) Wrap the output in distribution-free guarantees: conformal prediction, conformal risk control and selective abstention. Plain RLVR and RLHF actively destroy calibration. RL with proper-scoring-rule rewards repairs most of it in-domain, but leaves large out-of-domain error. Conformal and selective methods are the only ones that give a guarantee, and only under exchangeability.

### Cited Findings
- **RLCR** (Damani et al., arXiv 2507.16806, ICLR 2026). The reward is R = 1[y≡y*] − (q − 1[y≡y*])², with q the verbalised confidence. Theorem 1 says expected reward is maximised by calibrated q, and among calibrated answers the most-likely-correct one scores best. This holds for any *bounded* proper scoring rule. Log loss is unbounded and "can incentivize incorrect answers" — [RLCR paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.16806v2)
- RLCR setup: Qwen2.5-7B, GRPO, no KL term, trained on 20k HotpotQA-Modified or 15k Big-Math examples. In-domain, HotpotQA went from RLVR 63.0% / ECE 0.37 to RLCR 62.1% / ECE 0.03, and Big-Math from RLVR 72.9% / 0.26 to RLCR 72.7% / 0.10. Out-of-domain (HotpotQA-trained), RLCR got 56.2% / ECE 0.21, RLVR 53.9% / 0.46, and the base model 53.3% / 0.40 — [RLCR paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.16806v2)
- RLCR's stated limitations: absolute OOD calibration error stays high, the model can put high confidence on several contradictory answers (OOD confidences over exclusive answers often sum above 1), and ensembling gains are modest — [RLCR paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.16806v2)
- **Calibration-aware RL for decision-making LLMs** (Yaldiz et al., arXiv 2601.13284, Jan 2026). RLVR produces "extremely overconfident" models, while SFT is better calibrated, "even under distribution shift". The cause is the decision tokens, which act as extraction steps and carry no confidence. Their fix adjusts decision-token probabilities directly and cuts ECE by up to 9 points while keeping RLVR accuracy — [arXiv 2601.13284](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.13284)
- **RLHF causes verbalised overconfidence** (Leng et al., ICLR 2025). Reward models favour high-confidence responses regardless of quality. The proposed fixes, PPO-M and CDPO, recalibrate the reward and reduce ECE without losing accuracy — [arXiv 2410.09724](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.09724)
- Laya-style RLCD (the policy outputs a distribution, the reward is log + spherical + RPS, then post-hoc temperature scaling) is the decision-model-native version of the same idea — [HF blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/javadtaghia/rlcd-are-we-moving-from-ai-models-that-generate-an)
- **Verbalised vs token-probability confidence:** the evidence is mixed. One course summary says verbalised confidence often beats raw logits for RLHF models — [Lecture notes](https://teapot123.github.io/files/CSE_5610_Fall25/Lecture_14_Calibration.pdf). Decision-model products sidestep the question by reading a probability distribution over enumerated options directly, with no generated tokens — [Liquid guide](https://docs.liquid.ai/guides/decision-model-guide)
- Background, not re-fetched: pretrained LMs are fairly well calibrated on multiple-choice token probabilities, and RLHF degrades this ([Kadavath et al. 2022, arXiv 2207.05221](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221); [GPT-4 technical report, arXiv 2303.08774](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774)). For RLHF models, verbalised confidence can be better calibrated than conditional token probabilities ([Tian et al. 2023, arXiv 2305.14975](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14975)). Temperature scaling is the standard single-parameter post-hoc fix ([Guo et al. 2017, arXiv 1706.04599](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)).
- **Post-hoc temperature scaling in practice:** refitting Laya's temperature cut ECE from 0.466 to 0.081 — [HF blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/javadtaghia/rlcd-are-we-moving-from-ai-models-that-generate-an). Recalibrating Jev on local labels cut calibration error 3.3x — [arXiv 2609.24052](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24052)
- **Selective prediction with guarantees, "Trust or Escalate"** (arXiv 2407.18370). It estimates judge confidence ("Simulated Annotators"), abstains below a threshold, and cascades from cheap models (Mistral-7B) to stronger ones. On a Chatbot Arena subset where GPT-4 alone almost never reaches 80% human agreement, the cascade guarantees over 80% agreement at almost 80% coverage — [arXiv 2407.18370](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18370)
- **Conformal risk control for judges:** SCOPE (arXiv 2602.13110) calibrates an acceptance threshold so that error among *accepted* pairwise judgements is at most α, with a finite-sample guarantee under exchangeability — [SCOPE](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2602.13110.md). Multi-expert CRC (arXiv 2608.26529, EMNLP 2026) extends this to judge panels and defers abstentions to humans — [arXiv 2608.26529](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26529). Randomized and bootstrapped CRC frames a single ship/abstain/regenerate/escalate gate — [arXiv 2509.23007](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.23007v1). Conformal abstention bounds hallucination risk while minimising abstention — [arXiv 2405.01563](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.01563v1). Background: the general CRC framework is in [Angelopoulos et al., arXiv 2208.02814](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02814).

### Inferences
- For OrderGuard, the evidence favours a layered recipe. Train or choose a model whose objective rewards proper-scoring-rule calibration (RLCR-style Brier reward, or a decision model). Recalibrate post hoc on OrderGuard's own labelled actions (temperature or isotonic). Then set the auto-allow / escalate / block thresholds with conformal risk control so that "error among auto-approved actions ≤ α" is a stated, auditable property. That matches the review-queue design closely.
- A bounded scoring rule (Brier, spherical) matters for the reward. Pure log loss is unbounded and can be gamed.
- An SFT or classifier-head baseline should be the control. The HF critique and Yaldiz et al. both suggest RL is not obviously better than SFT for calibration, especially under shift.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head paper compares RL-for-calibration, SFT plus temperature scaling, and conformal wrapping on the same judge task.
- No evidence was found on calibration of multi-question outputs that are *combined in code*, e.g. whether products of per-question probabilities stay calibrated. AA's Paperclip uses the product, but its accuracy at the document level is not reported.

## 3. How are reward models, generative verifiers, PRMs and LLM judges built, how well calibrated are they, and how do they fail?

### Takeaway
Judges come in four families. Discriminative reward models put a scalar head on an LM. Generative verifiers are trained with next-token prediction to output Yes/No plus a rationale, with P(Yes) as the score. Process reward models score individual steps. Prompted LLM-as-judge needs no training. Generative verifiers clearly beat discriminative RMs on verification. But every family is exploitable: by superficial "master key" tokens, by position and verbosity bias, by step inflation, and by over-optimisation. On hard, objectively labelled pairs, even strong prompted judges are near chance. Domain judges (finance, for example) are built by adding domain rubrics, knowledge checks and expert calibration on top of these families.

### Cited Findings
- **Generative verifiers (GenRM)** (Zhang et al., arXiv 2408.15240, ICLR 2025) are trained with next-token prediction jointly on verification and solution generation, with CoT rationales and majority voting. Best-of-N gains over discriminative RMs: GSM8K 73% → 93.4%, algorithmic tasks 5% → 45.3%, easy-to-hard MATH 28% → 44.6%. GenRM also beats DPO verifiers and prompted LLM judges, and it scales with model size and test-time compute — [arXiv 2408.15240](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15240)
- Generative verifiers are themselves an attack surface, "a model that can be gamed" — [Survey of AI research agents, arXiv 2608.05179](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.05179)
- **"One Token to Fool LLM-as-a-Judge"** (arXiv 2507.08794, NeurIPS 2025). Superficial inputs such as ":" or "Thought process:" make generative RMs award false-positive rewards, with up to ~90% FPR reported, including on GPT-o1 and Claude-4. CoT prompting and majority voting do not fix it reliably. Training on 20k synthetic negatives ("Master-RM") brings FPR to near zero on the tested benchmarks — [arXiv 2507.08794](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08794)
- **PRM stress tests** (EST-PRM, arXiv 2606.00437). Qwen2.5-Math-PRM is most affected by step inflation, at a 47.6 ± 4.3% inflation rate, and confidence-based perturbations distort reward calibration — [EST-PRM](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.00437)
- Directly optimising aggregated PRM rewards produces reward hacking: PRM score rises while verifier score stays flat. VeriGate gates process rewards behind a verifier to prevent this — [search result, arXiv 2605.30451](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.30451) (snippet only)
- When a verifier is used as an optimisation target, its reported gain becomes an overestimate and its release calibration "stops holding". No shared benchmark yet scores verifier exploitability — [Verifier survey, arXiv 2609.09250](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.09250)
- **JudgeBench** (arXiv 2410.12784, ICLR 2025) uses response pairs labelled by objective correctness across knowledge, reasoning, math and code. Many strong judges, including GPT-4o, are "only slightly better than random guessing" — [arXiv 2410.12784](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12784)
- **RewardBench 2** has 1,865 mostly unseen human prompts across 6 domains, best-of-4 scoring, and a ties subset meant to test calibration among equally valid answers — [summary via search](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.19544) (secondary; background: [RewardBench 2, arXiv 2506.01937](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01937))
- **Bias in 2026 judges** (arXiv 2606.19544): test-retest reliability above 0.95 coexists with severe position bias (above 0.10) in two production judges. Verbosity bias has largely fallen (all 21 models below 0.011, against 20–40% variance in 2023 reports). JudgeBench separates judges 4.5x more sharply than MT-Bench — [arXiv 2606.19544](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.19544) (single preprint)
- Background, not re-fetched: position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases were first documented in [Zheng et al. 2023, MT-Bench/Chatbot Arena, arXiv 2306.05685](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685) and [Wang et al. 2023, arXiv 2305.17926](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926). Reward-model over-optimisation scaling laws are in [Gao, Schulman & Hilton 2022, arXiv 2210.10760](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10760). The process supervision and PRM800K origin is [Lightman et al. 2023, arXiv 2305.20050](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050). The taxonomy of judge benchmarks (general, bias, challenging, domain) is in the [Gu et al. survey, arXiv 2411.15594](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.15594v6).
- **Finance-domain judges:** Fin-PRM (IJCAI 2026) is a finance-specific PRM trained on 3k trajectories. Its labels combine Monte Carlo rollouts, LLM evaluation and financial knowledge checks, and the authors argue that pure LLM-as-judge in finance is "opaque and uninterpretable" — [Fin-PRM](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.15202v1). Fin-RATE (SEC filings, 17 LLMs) targets judges confined to generic axes like faithfulness — [arXiv 2602.07294](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.07294v3). FinResearchBench II uses rubric scoring by a 3-LLM panel, checked against 3 human experts on a sample — [FinResearchBench II](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.12252.md)
- Medical, legal and code domain judges: not researched in depth this session (see Gaps).

### Inferences
- OrderGuard's judge is mostly fed agent-written evidence and rationales, which is exactly the input channel that master-key and injection attacks use. The judge should score structured, system-derived features (positions, mandate terms, prices, history) and treat agent prose as untrusted, low-weight evidence. It also needs adversarial negatives (Master-RM style) in training.
- Generative-verifier designs (Yes/No probability with rationale) are the strongest family on accuracy. Decision-model designs (no rationale, distribution only) are the fastest and resist rationale-based hacking by construction, but they give less explanation for the review queue. A hybrid fits well: a fast typed decision score, plus a generative rationale only for escalated cases.
- The swarm risk model's "can only tighten" property is a structural guard against verifier over-optimisation. A judge's probability should be allowed to block or escalate, never to loosen a mandate.

### Gaps
- No published calibration (ECE or Brier) for RewardBench 2 or JudgeBench judges was retrieved. Those benchmarks measure accuracy far more than calibration.
- Medical, legal and code domain judges were not covered (search budget). Background candidates to check: Prometheus-style rubric judges, code execution verifiers.
- No finance-specific *calibration* benchmark for judges was found.

## 4. How do these models behave under distribution shift and adversarial inputs?

### Takeaway
Calibration learned in-domain degrades substantially out of domain. RLCR's OOD ECE of 0.21–0.25 is roughly 2–7x its in-domain value, though it remains better than RLVR's 0.46–0.49. RL-trained models are the most overconfident under shift, while SFT holds up better. Conformal guarantees break when exchangeability breaks. Judges are highly exploitable by superficial tokens and by step inflation, and no decision-model vendor has published adversarial robustness results. The only systematic perturbation test of decision models is AA-Symmetry, where answer consistency across reframings ranged from 67% to 99%.

### Cited Findings
- RLCR OOD: ECE 0.21 (HotpotQA-trained) and 0.25 (Big-Math-trained), against in-domain 0.03 and 0.10. RLVR OOD is 0.46 and 0.49. Absolute OOD error "remains high" — [RLCR paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.16806v2)
- SFT keeps better calibration than RLVR "even under distribution shift" — [arXiv 2601.13284](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.13284)
- Conformal and CRC judge guarantees hold only under exchangeability between calibration and test data. The papers state this explicitly — [SCOPE](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2602.13110.md); [arXiv 2608.26529](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26529)
- Verifier calibration stops holding once the verifier becomes an optimisation target — [arXiv 2609.09250](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.09250)
- Master-key tokens give up to ~90% false positives across frontier judges — [arXiv 2507.08794](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08794). Step inflation makes PRMs inflate scores in 47.6% of cases — [EST-PRM](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.00437)
- Decision-model consistency under reordered options, swapped labels and negation (AA-Symmetry): Mammoth 99%, Drex Max 91%, GLiDE 88%, Clef 67% — [AA v2](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/decision-index-v2)
- In Jev's field study, calibration needed local recalibration (3.3x error reduction), and each model must be audited separately — [arXiv 2609.24052](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24052)
- Background, not re-fetched: under dataset shift, calibration of all uncertainty methods degrades, and post-hoc temperature scaling fitted in-distribution does not transfer ([Ovadia et al. 2019, arXiv 1906.02530](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02530)). Pre-trained transformers stay comparatively better calibrated OOD than non-pretrained models ([Desai & Durrett 2020, arXiv 2003.07892](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07892)).

### Inferences
- Markets are non-stationary by definition (regime shifts), and agents may adapt to the judge, which is adversarial shift. OrderGuard should therefore: (a) monitor live calibration (Brier and reliability per segment on resolved outcomes); (b) recalibrate on a rolling window; (c) use conformal thresholds with drift detection and fall back to escalation when drift is detected; (d) keep the deterministic mandate checks as the hard floor, so that judge miscalibration can only cost review load, never loosen a limit.
- An AA-Symmetry-style invariance suite (the same trade described differently, reordered evidence, negated questions) is a cheap, necessary acceptance test for any System One candidate.

### Gaps
- No study was found on calibration of decision models (Jev, d1 and peers) under distribution shift or prompt injection.
- No finance-specific evidence on judge calibration under regime shift.
- Adaptive-adversary results (an agent optimising against a fixed judge) for typed decision models are absent.

## 5. What do small specialised judges cost and how fast are they, compared with frontier LLM judges?

### Takeaway
Specialised decision models and small judges run at 30–300 ms and roughly $14–$1,000 per million tasks. Frontier LLM judges at max reasoning take 11–33 s and $1.5k–$6k+ per million tasks on the same benchmark. That is a 40–580x latency gap and up to ~450x on cost. Frontier models still lead on hard accuracy (Paperclip 86–92% against 63%). Independent tests show small judges trailing frontier ones on hard tasks, and most parity claims come from vendors.

### Cited Findings
- AA Decision Index v2 latency per task: decision models 56–275 ms (Jev 1.14 92 ms), against GPT-6 Luna Max 11.34 s and Claude Haiku 5.5 Max 32.65 s. Mammoth 27B is ~118x faster than GPT-6 Luna and within 4 points of it. Cost per 1M tasks: $14–$859 for decision models against $1,530–$6,240 for frontier models. Billing per document vs per question matters — [AA v2](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/decision-index-v2)
- Laya is marketed at ~33 ms per decision, locally — [El Solitario](https://elsolitario.org/en/2026/09/19/laya-convai-decision-engine-33ms/); [eesel](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/laya-ai)
- Jev is claimed at 40–200x faster than frontier LLMs (vendor) — [DigitalOcean](https://www.digitalocean.com/resources/articles/what-is-jev)
- Vendor estimates for evaluation workloads: frontier judges at ~$10k–50k per 1M evals and 1–3 s latency, against small judges at ~$200–1,000 and 15–150 ms — [Galileo](https://galileo.ai/eval-engineering-book/scaling-evaluation-infrastructure). Luna-2 (single-token small-LM evaluator) claims >80x cheaper and >20x faster than LLM judges at matched accuracy on safety and hallucination tasks (vendor paper) — [Luna-2, arXiv 2602.18583](https://arxiv.deeppaper.ai/papers/2602.18583v1)
- Patronus Glider (small judge) claims parity with models 17x larger at ~1 s latency (vendor). An independent test by Galtea found it best among small judges but well below GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and it needs 16.1 GiB at inference — [Galtea](https://galtea.ai/blog/exploring-state-of-the-art-llms-as-judges); [Kowatek](https://blog.kowatek.com/?p=89153)
- Prometheus 2 (7B) reaches ~0.55–0.67 Pearson correlation with GPT-4 judgements and runs on ~16 GB VRAM. M-Prometheus reportedly surpasses it — [Galileo SLM list](https://galileo.ai/blog/best-small-language-models-for-ai-evaluation); [PromptLayer](https://www.promptlayer.com/glossary/prometheus-eval) (secondary)
- Cascades recover most of the quality cheaply. Trust or Escalate starts with Mistral-7B and escalates only when uncertain, guaranteeing over 80% human agreement at ~80% coverage — [arXiv 2407.18370](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18370)

### Inferences
- For a pre-trade gate in the order path, only the specialised-model regime (under ~300 ms) fits inline. Frontier judges fit only asynchronous escalation or the review queue. A cascade (a small calibrated System One model inline, then a frontier generative judge, then a human), with thresholds set by conformal risk control, is the design best supported by current evidence.
- Since the frontier lead on accuracy is real, a domain-specific System One should be measured against a frontier-judge baseline on OrderGuard's own labelled set before claiming superiority. AA's Decision Score (50 × (1 + Acc − Brier)) is a reasonable single headline metric to adopt.

### Gaps
- No public latency or cost figures for d1 or d1-ultra beyond the $0.042 per M input tokens snippet. Liquid's own docs give none.
- Latency and cost of domain-specific (finance) judges were not found.
